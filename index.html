Well-Formed Scale Explorer

Help

What This App Does

Well-Formed Scale Explorer builds and plays well-formed scales from either generator data or scale-step data.

The scale updates automatically as valid values are entered, making it easy to modify, compare, and reshape scales.

Build Method

Generator Build

Specify the scale by period p and generator g in frequency ratios, or by p and logp(g).

Choose a compatible cardinality N from the available values.

Scale Step Build

Specify the scale by:

  • period
  • cardinality
  • number of A steps
  • either the relative proportion A:B in logarithmic units (equivalently, cents), or the size of A as a fraction of the period

Valid builder edits update the scale automatically. Incomplete or invalid entries leave the last valid scale in place. Build Scale or Enter rebuilds immediately. Undo and Redo beside the build controls return to previous scales; typing in one field counts as one edit until you leave the field or confirm it, and each completed circle drag counts as one edit. Standard Undo/Redo shortcuts work outside text fields.

Base Frequency

The base frequency sets the sounding register of the scale.

You may enter:

  • a note name in scientific pitch notation, such as F3, C4, Bb4
  • or a frequency in Hz

Information Panels

Cardinalities

  • N: total number of scale tones in the period
  • #A, #B: numbers of occurrences of the step types
  • A/B: ratio of the step sizes measured in cents (equivalently, in logarithmic units)

Values

Shows p, g, A, and B as frequency ratios and cents values.

Explorer

The Explorer can be shown either as a Keyboard or as a Scale Circle.

The header displays:

  • Step pattern using A and B
  • Cycle pattern using a and b
  • Cycle folding using x and y
  • Generator span, the cycle corresponding to generator order

A and B retain their construction identities; either can be larger. The cycle interval a contains one more A step and one fewer B step than b, so a−b = A−B in logarithmic units. The folding letters x and y mean upward and downward motion through scale order, including closure. Parenthesized counts after a cycle word follow its letter order: (a,b) or (x,y). The interval table below the cycle controls shows the Large and Small intervals as frequency ratios and cents values. A selected coset may contain only one type. In the circle view, the same colors are used on the cycle segments.

Keyboard

The keyboard allows a range of two full periods for demonstration. It shows:

  • white keys for scale tones
  • black keys for auxiliary tones between adjacent scale tones

Click a key to play it; quick mouse clicks ring for one beat and can overlap. Hold several computer keys, or touch several screen keys, to play a chord. Held notes release independently.

The computer keyboard is active in Keyboard view:

  • lower-case keys play white keys
  • shifted keys play the black key to the right

Scale Circle

The Scale Circle places the notes of the scale around a circle, with the current tonic at the top.

Click a point to play it. In Scale Circle view, Shift-click plays that note one period higher.

Any nonzero scale degree can also be click-dragged to continuously transform the scale. As the point is dragged, the two step sizes change while the cardinality and generator span remain fixed, producing a continuous family of well-formed scales. The scale can be transformed up to a limiting degenerate well-formed scale in either direction. An ordinary click still plays the note.

Mode

The mode control selects the tonic within the scale.

Mode order steps through modes either:

  • in scale-step order
  • in generator order

Keyboard Labels

  • note numbers
  • Hz
  • Hz/Base
  • cents

Cycle Controls

The cycle selector chooses a cycle through the scale degrees. Special cases include:

  • 1: step order
  • N-1: reverse step order
  • Generator span: generator order
  • N - Generator span: reverse generator order

If the chosen cycle step is not relatively prime to N, the cycle breaks into cosets. In that case a Coset selector appears.

Tempo is a metronome marking in beats per minute (BPM), with one cycle note per quarter-note beat. For example, ♩ = 120 plays two notes per second; ♩ = 60 plays one note per second. Higher BPM values play faster. Change tempo while stopped; changing it during playback stops the cycle, ready to restart at the new tempo. Volume adjusts playback level. Press Escape to stop sound immediately.

Timbre selects piano, marimba, pipe organ, clarinet, or a pure sine wave. Recordings load on first use. Hold a keyboard key (on screen or computer) to sustain; piano and marimba decay naturally. Cycle notes begin one per beat, with a short release at the next note.

Instrument recordings: Versilian Community Sample Library (piano, marimba, organ) and VSCO 2 Community Edition (clarinet), released under CC0.

Special Case: Degenerate Well-Formed Scales

Every equal tempered scale is understood to be a degenerate well-formed scale. Whenever logp(g) is rational, such a scale will be the largest cardinality of its hierarchy. Such scales do not transport back predictably from Scale Step Build to Generator Build.

About

About Well-Formed Scales

1. What Is a Well-Formed Scale?

A well-formed scale is a musical scale constructed from two musical intervals: a generator and an interval or periodicity, called the period. The generator is repeatedly applied to produce a succession of pitches, while the period establishes the interval within which those pitches are organized, treating pitches separated by one or more periods as equivalent. Well-formedness occurs at particular cardinalities, where generator order and scale order have a regular relationship: the generating interval consistently spans the same number of scale steps. The paradigmatic example is Pythagorean tuning, in which the generator is the perfect fifth (3:2) and the perfect octave (2:1) is the period.

Choose a period p > 1 and a generator g > 0, expressed as frequency ratios. In logarithmic coordinates, write

τ = logp(g) = log(g)/log(p).

Successive applications of the generator give the points

0, τ, 2τ, … mod 1.

For a chosen cardinality N, take the first N points, nτ mod 1 for n = 0, 1, …, N−1, and arrange them in scale order around the period. If τ is irrational, the Three-Gap Theorem (also called the Three-Distance Theorem) states that the gaps between consecutive points have at most three distinct sizes; when there are three, the largest is the sum of the other two. At particular cardinalities only two sizes occur; these are the nondegenerate well-formed scales. Their cardinalities are determined by the continued-fraction expansion of τ, through its convergents and intermediate convergents.

If τ = u/v in lowest terms, generation produces v distinct pitch classes. The hierarchy terminates in this equal-interval scale, having only one step size—a degenerate well-formed scale.

For example, with the perfect fifth g = 3/2 as generator and the octave p = 2/1 as period,

τ = log2(3/2).

The convergents and intermediate convergents of the continued fraction of τ determine the well-formed hierarchy, whose cardinalities include

2, 3, 5, 7, 12, 17, 29, 41, 53, …

The familiar pentatonic, diatonic, and chromatic scales therefore belong to the same generated hierarchy.

A particularly useful characterization of well-formedness is that the generator always spans the same number of scale steps. In the diatonic scale, for example, every generating perfect fifth spans four scale steps. More generally, well-formedness imposes regularity not merely on the individual scale steps but throughout the interval structure of the scale. In a nondegenerate well-formed scale, intervals spanning any given number of scale steps from 1 through N−1 occur in exactly two sizes. In a degenerate well-formed scale, each such span has only one size. For an arbitrary scale, merely having two sizes of scale step is not sufficient for well-formedness; for the consecutive-generator construction described above, however, exactly two step sizes identify the nondegenerate well-formed stages.

2. Well-Formed Scales and Moments of Symmetry

The theory of well-formed scales is closely related to Erv Wilson's theory of Moments of Symmetry (MOS). Wilson developed the MOS idea in the context of tuning theory, using repeated applications of a generator modulo a period to identify particularly symmetrical stages in the growth of a generated scale. The term Moment of Symmetry reflects the appearance, at particular cardinalities, of a highly regular two-size interval structure.

Norman Carey and David Clampitt introduced the term well-formed scale in their 1989 article “Aspects of Well-Formed Scales.” Their formulation emphasizes the relationship between generator order and scale order and develops the recursive organization of well-formed scales into hierarchies.

The MOS and well-formed approaches arose in somewhat different traditions—Wilson's primarily in tuning theory and well-formedness in mathematical music theory—but they describe closely related manifestations of the same underlying generated-scale structure. Under the usual conditions represented in this Explorer, the two theories identify the same characteristic scales, although extensions and generalizations of the concepts need not coincide in every context.

Continued fractions provide a natural mathematical description of this structure. Successive convergents and intermediate convergents of the generator determine the cardinalities at which the two-size organization occurs and thereby organize the successive members of a well-formed hierarchy.

3. Step Patterns, Cycle Patterns, Foldings, and Words

The Explorer displays several related combinatorial descriptions of a well-formed scale that show different aspects of the relationship between scale order and cyclic order.

Step Patterns

A nondegenerate well-formed scale has two scale-step types, labeled A and B in the Explorer. These labels retain their identities as their sizes change: A may be larger than, equal to, or smaller than B. A new Generator Build initially assigns A to the larger step; Scale Step Build uses the supplied A and B identities. Reading the steps successively around the scale from the selected tonic gives a binary word: the Step pattern using A and B. At A = B, the scale has one step size, but the construction labels are retained when available.

Changing the mode changes the starting point of the circular step pattern, producing a different ordering of the same collection of steps.

Cycle Patterns

Scale-step order is only one way of traversing the notes. The Explorer permits the user to choose other cycles through the scale degrees and displays the resulting Cycle pattern using a and b. The letters a and b identify the two compositions of intervals encountered along the selected cycle: for the same generic span, a contains one more A step and one fewer B step than b. Thus a−b = A−B in logarithmic units. This common difference across generic spans follows from the balanced structure of well-formed scales, which also gives Myhill’s Property. The letters retain these identities as the sizes cross or become equal. At most two sizes occur; a selected cycle can have only one size. A chosen span visits all N scale degrees in one cycle when it is relatively prime to N. Otherwise, it divides the scale into several shorter cycles, and the Explorer permits selection of a coset.

Thus the Explorer allows the interval structure of a well-formed scale to be examined under different cyclic traversals rather than only in ordinary scale order.

Generator order is especially important. If the scale has cardinality N and its generator spans k scale steps, generator order is the orbit (with k relatively prime to N)

0, k, 2k, 3k, … mod N.

In other words, the generator acts on the scale degrees in ZN by

i ↦ i + k mod N.

This modular formulation expresses directly the relationship between generator order and scale order that lies at the heart of well-formedness.

Cycle Foldings

The Explorer also displays the Cycle folding using x and y. The letter x records an upward motion and y a downward motion through scale order, including the closing motion. These direction labels are independent of the interval-composition labels a and b.

For the generator cycle, the modular displacement k mod N can be represented by the two directed motions

+k

and

−(N−k).

Thus x and y may be understood as recording the upward and downward motions of the folded generator cycle. Over a complete cycle, the two occur N−k and k times, respectively, up to orientation and the assignment of the letters x and y.

For the seven-note diatonic scale, N = 7, and the perfect fifth has generator span k = 4. Generator order is therefore addition by 4 mod 7. Its two directed representatives are

+4 and −3.

The complete folding, closing back onto the initial scale degree, consequently contains three upward motions of four scale steps and four downward motions of three scale steps:

3(4) + 4(−3) = 0.

This closed-cycle convention differs from foldings that end at the next generated boundary pitch, as also discussed in the height-width literature.

The Explorer makes these relationships visible simultaneously: the Step pattern using A and B describes the scale in scale order; the Cycle pattern using a and b describes the intervals encountered under a chosen cyclic traversal; and the Cycle folding using x and y shows how that traversal folds through scale order.

Word Theory and Scale Theory

An intriguing development in the study of well-formed scales has been the convergence of mathematical music theory and combinatorics on words. These subjects developed largely independently, yet they were found to share remarkably similar structures, particularly those involving continued fractions, cyclic arrangements, and balanced patterns.

The two step sizes of a nondegenerate well-formed scale, represented by A and B, form a binary word when read in scale order. When the scale begins with the initial generated pitch (n = 0, so nτ = 0), the resulting step pattern is a Christoffel word—a finite binary word characterized by a particularly regular distribution of its two letters. The step patterns of the other modes are its conjugates, or cyclic rotations. Thus the relationship between well-formed scales and Christoffel words is not merely one of resemblance: the same mathematical objects arise in both theories.

For irrational generators, the related theory of Sturmian words provides an infinite counterpart. Continued fractions play a central role in both theories, governing the organization of generated scales and the combinatorial structure of their associated words.

The relationship extends beyond step patterns. The cycle patterns and foldings displayed in the Explorer reveal further connections between the geometry of generated scales and the arithmetic of binary words. Subsequent research, including work on height-width duality and partitioned factors, has explored these correspondences in greater detail.
